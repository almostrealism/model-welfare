The measurement of time has always depended on finding processes that repeat
at a steady rate. Early astronomers used the apparent motion of the sun across
the sky, dividing the day into hours whose length varied with the seasons.
Water clocks improved on sundials by working at night, though their flow
changed with temperature and the level of water remaining in the vessel.
Mechanical escapements introduced a new principle: rather than tracking a
continuous flow, they counted discrete oscillations, and the regularity of the
count came to matter more than the size of the interval being counted.
Pendulum clocks refined this idea by tying the oscillation to a physical
constant, the local acceleration of gravity, and for two centuries they set
the standard for precision. Their weakness was portability; a pendulum at sea
loses its regularity with every swell.

The marine chronometer solved that problem with a balance wheel and spring
whose period depended on the spring's stiffness rather than on gravity, and
its arrival transformed navigation. A ship's longitude could now be computed
by comparing local solar time with the time kept by a clock set at a reference
port. An error of a few seconds per day translated into a positional error of
several kilometers over a long voyage, so the history of the chronometer is a
history of compensating for small disturbances: temperature changes that
altered the spring, friction that varied with the oil's age, and the slow
settling of metal parts under repeated stress.

Quartz oscillators replaced springs with a vibrating crystal driven and read
electrically, raising the count rate from a few beats per second to tens of
thousands, and atomic standards raised it again by locking an electronic
oscillator to a transition frequency of an atom, a quantity believed to be the
same everywhere and at all times. Each step in this sequence shares a common
structure: a periodic process, a counter, and a scheme for isolating the
process from influences that would change its rate. The influences never
disappear entirely. They are measured, modeled, and corrected, and the
residual after correction defines the accuracy of the clock.

A similar structure appears in the calibration of any measuring instrument.
A balance is compared against reference masses, a thermometer against fixed
points such as the freezing and boiling of water at known pressure, a
voltmeter against a standard cell. The comparison does not make the
instrument perfect; it makes the instrument's imperfection known, which for
practical purposes is nearly as good. An instrument whose error is stable and
documented can be used far outside the laboratory that characterized it,
because every reading can be adjusted after the fact. What cannot be
tolerated is an error that drifts silently, changing between the moment of
calibration and the moment of use.

For this reason, calibration procedures usually end by stating not only the
measured correction but also the conditions under which it was obtained and
the period for which it may be trusted. The statement of conditions is as
important as the number itself. A correction measured at one temperature, on
one bench, with one operator, is a fact about that occasion; whether it
transfers to another occasion is a hypothesis, and laboratories differ mainly
in how carefully they test such hypotheses before relying on them. The
disciplines that depend on precise measurement have accumulated, over
centuries, a shared etiquette for this testing: measure again under the new
conditions, compare against the old, and treat any unexplained difference as
a finding rather than a nuisance. Instruments, like the theories they serve,
earn trust not by never being wrong but by being wrong in ways that are
understood, bounded, and honestly recorded.
